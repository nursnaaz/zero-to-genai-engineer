The one idea

Calibration: when it says 80%,
it is right 80% of the time.

🌦️ The weather forecaster test. Collect every day she said "70% chance of rain". If it rained on about 70% of those days, she is calibrated.

This chart is a reliability diagram. Bars on the dashed line mean the confidence is honest. Bars below the line mean the model is overconfident. In this example it said 95% but was right only 66% of the time.
Calibration is not accuracy. A model can be 60% accurate and perfectly calibrated, as long as it says 60%. What calibration gives you is knowing when to trust each answer.

Bar heights are illustrative, for teaching.

Where RLCD fits

Same pretrained model.
Three ways to reward it.

TypeSafe's own wording: RLCD optimizes for "calibrated decisions: answers with epistemically honest probabilities on System One tasks." RLCD is a training method. It is not an architecture.
Why a new method was needed

Preference training squeezes the probabilities
into a confident spike.

Spread across several plausible answers.

Bar heights are illustrative.

Mode dropping. RLHF teaches the model to favour one style of answer, and the probability of every other answer drains away. What is left reflects what raters liked, not how often the answer is right.

RLVR pays 1 for a correct answer and 0 otherwise. The best strategy is to always bet everything on your top guess. The OpenJev-RLCD paper finds RLVR makes reasoning models "sharply overconfident."
RLCD pays for the whole distribution being honest. Being 100% sure about a wrong answer is the worst thing it can do.
The reward: a proper scoring rule

Brier score: penalty = (confidence − truth)²

Truth is 1 if the answer was right and 0 if it was wrong. Drag the slider and watch how being confidently wrong explodes.

"Proper" means the lowest average penalty comes from reporting what you actually believe. Bluffing never pays off over many examples.
TypeSafe has not published Jev's exact reward. Brier and log loss are what the open reproduction uses, and what outside observers suspect Jev uses.

Why the model learns to be honest

Over many examples, the lowest penalty
comes from telling the truth.

On this kind of input the model is really right 70% of the time
Average Brier penalty if it always says…

Bar heights are stretched to make small differences visible. Read the numbers.

Average penalty = q·(1−p)² + (1−q)·p²

q = how often it is really right. p = what it says.

The green bar always lands where p = q. Saying 100% when you are right 70% of the time costs more. So does saying 50%. Gradient descent drifts the model toward honest numbers.
This is the whole trick: with the right reward, an honest model is the one that loses the least. That is how the training loop makes "80% sure" mean "right 80% of the time."

GRPO and RLCD had almost the same accuracy (91.8% vs 92.2%). The big difference is how many answers you can safely automate: GRPO's confidence could not separate its right answers from its wrong ones. LLM work grows with every word it writes. Jev writes no words: the answers are a fixed list you defined, so it can score all of them at once. TypeSafe credits this design for its claimed 70–500 ms responses, versus 3–329 s end to end for frontier LLMs.
Architecture · part 2 · be honest with students

What is inside Jev?
Known, unknown, and guessed.

Confirmed by TypeSafe

  • Transformer-based
  • Starts from a pretrained language model, then RLCD post-training
  • "New model architecture" with a parallel, hardware-aware sampler
  • No text generation: typed answers only, all questions in parallel
  • Not trained on customer requests or responses

Not disclosed

  • Base model and parameter count
  • Encoder, decoder, or something new?
  • The exact reward / loss function
  • Training data details and calibration curves
  • No paper yet. The CEO said the architecture is "close to the chest for now"

Popular guesses

  • BERT-style bidirectional encoder with classification heads
  • Built on top of an open-weight LLM
  • Brier or log loss inside the reward
  • Treat these as hypotheses, not facts
Common myth: "Jev has a separate confidence head." Jev returns one probability distribution per question. The API then computes choice and confidence from it with fixed formulas (next slides).

confidence = (pmax − 1/n) / (1 − 1/n)
= (0.82 − 0.25) / 0.75 = 0.76

0 means a flat, random-guess distribution. 1 means all probability on one option. For score questions the answer is the probability-weighted average, and confidence measures how tightly the probabilities cluster.
By the formula, confidence only measures how concentrated the probabilities are. Whether high confidence really means "usually correct" is the calibration promise, so check it on your own labelled data.

The production pattern

Jev decides. The LLM writes.
A human sees only the hard cases.

1
Give Jev a way outAdd "None" or "Other" so it never has to force a bad pick.
2
Send the unsure ones to a personStart at about 60% and tune it from real results.
3
Check before you trustRun it on a labelled set and compare accuracy at each confidence level.
4
Pin the versionUse jev-1.13.0, not jev-latest, once you've tuned a threshold.

Claims vs measurement

Those were their claims.
Here is what I measured.

Same three of our use cases, same states, same options, same gold labels, run against all three systems. 88 decisions each. Strands ran locally on an M2 Pro; Jev and the Decisions API were called over the internet from Dubai.

Read this before you quote a winner. McNemar exact test on the paired results: Jev vs Strands p=1.000, Jev vs Luna p=0.375, Strands vs Luna p=0.219. None of the accuracy gaps are statistically significant at 88 items. The three systems disagreed on only 7 of 74 choice decisions. The honest conclusion is that they are in the same accuracy band, and you should choose on latency, hosting and cost instead.

Latency is not like for like: Strands has no network hop but modest hardware; their own figure is ~115 ms on an RTX 3090. Jev cost uses TypeSafe's published $0.042/1M input; Luna uses OpenAI's $0.10/1M input, output unbilled on both.

Jev separates hardest, and that is the point of RLCD. A confidence number is only useful if being unsure actually predicts being wrong. Jev drops to 37.5% below the gate, so the threshold carries real information. The other two stay above 80% when unsure, which means their low-confidence bucket is safer but far less informative, so you would send work to humans that did not need it.
